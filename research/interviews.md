# Qualitative Research · Operator Interviews

> Two public interviews with large short-term rental operators in Spain, analysed for the case study. Both sit outside the core persona (small hosts, 1–5 listings) but sharpen the solution, the Pro tier and the risk register. Complements [`qualitative-reddit.md`](qualitative-reddit.md).

_Both are public media pieces with interested speakers. Treated as directional signals, not representative evidence. Legal interpretations quoted are the speakers' opinions, not settled law._

_____

## Interview 1 · Rubén Zaballos, owner of 150 homes

**Source:** *Más Vale Tarde*, laSexta (YouTube, ~October 2026). Clip of about 4 minutes; the transcript ends mid-question.

**Profile:** large owner; most homes in rural towns of around 10,000 inhabitants.

**What he says**
- Moved from long-term to tourist rental because of **legal uncertainty**, not yield: fear that tenants stop paying and that contracts can't be ended. His last long-term contract was signed three years ago.
- Uses tourist rental **defensively** while he sells his whole portfolio, to avoid squatting risk; has moved his business to Mexico, where he says he has legal certainty. (The presenter disputes his tax comparison; not assessed here.)
- **Rural seasonality kills the tourist model:** full in July and August, empty the rest of the year, while community fees, utilities and year-round staff keep running. Estimates a yield "closer to 3% than to 5%" he'd get from long-term renting.
- Would accept the new decree's transparency (reference price, previous rent) if he rented long term.
- The presenter raises a useful point: with an election on 29 November, owners may pull homes off the market and wait.

**Implications**
- **New segment: the defensive host.** Uses tourist rental as protection against tenant law, not to maximise income. If tenant law changes (for example faster evictions under a new government), they may return to long-term renting and churn, regardless of how good the compliance product is. → Added to the roadmap risk register.
- **Seasonal mode switching for rural portfolios.** Tourist stays in peak season, mid-term stays (remote workers, professionals) the rest of the year, with compliance handled in both. → Strengthens the case for mid-term mode in rural destinations.
- **Remote operations matter more in rural areas**, where fixed costs eat the margin.

## Interview 2 · Jon Goitia, architect, investor and tourist operator (Madrid)

**Source:** Graciani Asesores y Abogados podcast (YouTube), recorded around **March 2025**, before the April 2025 Horizontal Property Law reform took effect. Both speakers operate tourist rentals; the podcast is run by a law firm. Clearly pro-industry.

**Profile:** around 400 homes created through change of use (commercial premises to housing) in Madrid, 2018–2020; now operates his own tourist units.

**What he says**
- His business logic: guests pay **per bed, not per square metre**.
- Now builds only **tourist apartments** (*apartamentos turísticos*) rather than tourist homes (*viviendas de uso turístico*): in the Community of Madrid 21 m² minimum instead of 40 m², in groups of at least 4 units (3 in Andalucía) with common ownership and management. In his view, many Andalusian towns limit tourist homes but not tourist apartments.
- **The tourist / seasonal boundary varies by region** (his reading): over one month is not tourist in Galicia; from 11 nights in the Comunitat Valenciana; 2 months in Andalucía; unclear in Madrid, where work and care stays are excluded from tourism law.
- In his reading, communities can ban tourist homes by a 3/5 vote, but not hostels, tourist apartments or seasonal rentals. Not confirmed by the Supreme Court.
- **Dates and documents decide outcomes.** A works licence for a tourist home, obtained before a community ban, protects the owner (backed by some provincial courts, not the Supreme Court). A community ban not recorded in the Land Registry doesn't bind a good-faith buyer. How the 2025 reform's grandfathering ("already carrying out the activity") will be read is uncertain: "the only thing we can say is that there is uncertainty".
- Guest trust: verified profiles and the risk of being banned from the platform keep guests in line; recommends accepting only verified guests with past bookings.
- His social argument (his opinion, not assessed): listing counts overstate tourist supply because tourist ads stay up while long-term ads come down once let; his units come from empty commercial premises, not homes; nearby shops say his guests keep them open.

**Implications**
- **Compliance is not yes/no: it depends on each unit's legal category.** The Compliance Hub must model the **legal category per unit** (tourist home, tourist apartment, seasonal, work stay) and the **regional stay thresholds** that move a booking from one regime to another, and flag bookings that change regime. → Added to the Compliance Hub MVP scope in the roadmap.
- **Evidence vault with dates.** Storing licences, applications, community minutes and certificates with timestamps can decide whether a host keeps the right to operate. → Added to the Compliance Hub MVP scope.
- **Professionalisation confirmed.** Professional operators already move to hotel-like categories with common management, as the two-tier analysis in [`04-scenarios`](../04-scenarios/scenarios.md) predicts. → Supports the Pro tier in LATER.
- **Positioning guardrail.** Category choice is legitimate compliance work, but the product must stay "compliance made easy", never a loophole finder.

## The common thread

| | Rubén Zaballos | Jon Goitia |
|---|---|---|
| **Portfolio** | 150 homes, mostly rural | Urban Madrid units from converted premises |
| **Strategy** | Defensive tourist rental while selling | Optimise the legal category and stay |
| **Yield** | ~3% rural, below long-term renting | Above long-term renting |
| **Decision driver** | Fear of tenants | Regulatory arbitrage |

Both decide on **legal certainty**, not yield alone. That matches the case's core reframe: hosts don't control their permission to operate.
